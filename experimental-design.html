EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

Design the experiment before you run it.

Turn a biological question into a testable hypothesis, a controlled experiment, and interpretable evidence.

THE CORE PRINCIPLE

Don't start with the technique. Start with the question.

A well-designed experiment connects the biological question to measurable variables, appropriate controls, replication, and an analysis plan.

TECHNIQUE SELECTION

The technique should answer the question.

Choose the method based on the biological measurement you need—not simply because it is familiar.

PCR

DNA Amplification

Target-specific amplification

Useful when the research question requires amplification or detection of a specific DNA sequence.

qPCR

Quantitative Measurement

Quantitative PCR

Used when the experimental question involves quantifying nucleic-acid targets under an appropriate experimental design.

WB

Protein Detection

Western Blot

Useful when the question concerns the presence or relative abundance of a specific protein.

IF

Spatial Localization

Immunofluorescence

Useful when spatial distribution or cellular localization is part of the biological question.

EVIDENCE FIRST

An experimental result is not automatically a biological conclusion.

Interpretation requires controls, appropriate replication, analysis, and context.

OBSERVATION

What did you actually measure?

Record the direct experimental observation without adding biological interpretation.

EVIDENCE

What supports the observation?

Consider controls, replicates, measurement quality, and the analysis used.

INTERPRETATION

What can the evidence reasonably support?

Keep the conclusion proportional to the experiment and its limitations.

SCIENTIFIC QUALITY

Before running the experiment, ask:

01 Is the research question specific?
02 Can the hypothesis be tested experimentally?
03 Are the controls appropriate?
04 Are biological replicates independent?
05 Is the analysis plan defined before looking at the result?

COMMON MISTAKES

Where experimental design often goes wrong

01

Choosing the technique first

A familiar technique is not necessarily the right measurement for the biological question.

02

Treating technical repeats as biological replicates

Repeated measurements of the same biological sample do not automatically create independent biological replicates.

03

Ignoring controls

Without appropriate controls, an observed difference may have alternative explanations.

04

Deciding the analysis after seeing the data

A predefined analysis strategy can reduce unnecessary analytical flexibility.
